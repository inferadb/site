SEC.01 The convergence

Two forces are consolidating authorization right now — and they're feeding each other.

Authorization wasn't ignored for a decade because the problem was unimportant. It was ignored because the workloads tolerated the tolerable. Human users making synchronous requests at human speeds. Application logs that auditors accepted because the alternative was nothing better.

Both conditions broke in the same 18 months.

AI agents broke the model.

A traditional API request triggers one or two permission checks. An AI agent action triggers thirty or more — one per principal it acts on behalf of, one per tool it invokes, one per artifact it touches. At the 5–50 millisecond per-check latency floor of Postgres-backed authorization systems, a single agent action takes seconds to authorize. The product is broken before any business logic runs.

ISACA framed it as "the looming authorization crisis." Cisco and Microsoft both shipped agent-authorization frameworks at RSA 2026. When two security incumbents move on the same topic on the same date, the category is consolidating. Enterprise security teams are now actively shopping for the layer.

Regulators caught up — to the same problem.

Three regulations now compel auditable, fine-grained access control with tamper-evident logs:

  • NIS2 (EU, effective 2024) — extends cybersecurity obligations to ~160,000 EU entities including critical-infrastructure operators.
  • DORA (EU financial sector, effective January 2025) — mandates auditable ICT risk controls for every financial firm operating in the EU.
  • EU AI Act (phased through 2026–2027) — requires record-keeping for high-risk AI systems, including who authorized what action.

US regulators are pulling in the same direction: NIST 800-53 Rev 5 access-enforcement controls, FedRAMP audit-log requirements, and the SEC's 2023 cyber-disclosure rule.

The intersection is the wedge.

The EU AI Act doesn't just regulate AI — it requires auditable authorization records for every high-risk AI system. NIS2 doesn't just require access controls — it requires tamper-evident ones. The agentic era and the regulatory cycle aren't separate timing triggers. The regulatory cycle is the regulators' response to the agentic era.

Every authorization vendor in the market addresses one half. We built the system that addresses both — at agent speed, with cryptographic audit by default. Every enterprise CISO has this on the 2026 budget.

SEC.02 The market

$43 billion by 2030. The under-invested half. No managed leader.

The IAM (identity and access management) market is approximately $26 billion today, projected to reach $43 billion by 2030 on industry analyst estimates. It splits roughly in half: authentication — proving who someone is — and authorization — deciding what they're allowed to do.

Authentication consolidated. Okta, Auth0 (acquired by Okta for $6.5 billion), Microsoft Entra, and a handful of regional incumbents now own most of it. Authorization did not. Total funding across pure-play authorization startups remains under $70 million — roughly one-tenth of Auth0's exit value alone. Every company that needs fine-grained access control still builds it themselves: bespoke role tables, scattered permission checks, audit logs nobody trusts.

That arrangement was tolerable when users were human and applications were monolithic. The convergence in SEC.01 has made it untenable for the next 18 months.

SEC.03 What we built

A managed authorization database — call an API, never operate the infrastructure.

InferaDB is a fully managed authorization service. Customers integrate by calling an API. We handle multi-region replication, zero-downtime upgrades, compliance posture, and monitoring. Underneath, it's a purpose-built database engine written in Rust — not a policy library bolted onto Postgres.

That architectural choice is the entire product. It produces three properties that the competitive set cannot match without rebuilding their storage layer:

  • Microsecond decisions. Permission checks run in 2.8 microseconds at the 99th percentile — roughly two thousand times faster than published competitor latencies. This is what makes AI agent workloads tractable.
  • Cryptographic audit by default. Every access decision is hash-chained and Merkle-verified. Auditors confirm the integrity of the log without trusting our infrastructure. No competitor offers this; for SOC 2, HIPAA, NIS2, and EU AI Act conformance it is the difference between a clean audit and a finding.
  • Cryptographic per-tenant isolation. Each customer's data is encrypted with its own key at the storage layer. Cross-tenant leakage isn't prevented by application logic; it's architecturally impossible. In healthcare, government, and financial services, this is the difference between selling and not selling.

Pricing is published, usage-based, and starts free — live at /pricing. The closest commercial competitor (Auth0 FGA, now Okta) is sales-led with no public pricing. Transparent pricing is itself a category wedge.

SEC.05 Why we win

A four-year structural lead, not a feature lead.

The competitive set in authorization is well-funded and partially established. Every vendor sells some variant of "fine-grained access control." None has the architecture to ship the three properties we ship — and none can add them without rebuilding their storage engine. The honest comparison:

Vendor Their position Why they can't replicate
Authzed / SpiceDB The Zanzibar sage. Open-source ReBAC engine over Postgres or CockroachDB. SOC 2 Type II shipped. Storage layer is a general-purpose database. Cryptographic audit and per-tenant cryptographic isolation require rewriting the engine — abandoning their existing customer base.
OpenFGA (CNCF) The OSS community standard. No commercial offering of its own. Auth0/Okta own commercial deployment. We have the founder lineage and an independent commercial path they don't.
Auth0 FGA (Okta) The incumbent's enterprise tier. Built directly on OpenFGA. Inherits OpenFGA's storage architecture — same Postgres-backed ceiling. Pricing now sales-led only — wedge for transparent-priced alternatives.
Cerbos Multi-paradigm policy engine (RBAC/ABAC/ReBAC/PBAC). Nine framework certifications. Ships today. Stateless policy engine — no relationship store, no cryptographic audit, no per-tenant isolation. Solves a different shape of problem than ours.
Permit.io / Oso Both pivoted to AI agent permissions / agent observability in 2026. Pivot signals the original positioning didn't hold. Both inherit general-purpose storage constraints. Both compete on agent surface, not architectural correctness.

The structural moat

Each of our three differentiating properties — microsecond latency, cryptographic audit, per-tenant cryptographic isolation — is a storage-layer architectural choice. They cannot be added to a competitor's product as a feature. They require rebuilding the storage engine — which is the entire product.

Cerbos is stateless and would have to invent a storage layer. SpiceDB and OpenFGA are bolted onto Postgres or CockroachDB and would have to rewrite the storage tier they currently rely on. Auth0 FGA inherits OpenFGA's storage. None can ship our properties without a multi-year engine rewrite that abandons their existing customers.

We have a four-to-five-year structural lead, not a feature lead. The architecture is the moat.

Open core plus managed cloud — the same playbook that built four $10B+ infrastructure companies.

This is the exact shape of the four most successful developer infrastructure companies of the past decade:

  • Auth0 — open core (now CNCF), managed identity cloud — exited to Okta for $6.5 billion in 2021.
  • Datadog — open-source agents, managed observability cloud — public, ~$50 billion market cap.

  • MongoDB — open-source database, managed Atlas cloud — public, ~$25 billion market cap.

The pattern is consistent: open-core infrastructure becomes the default for its category, and the managed cloud captures the value. Authorization is the next category waiting for it.

SEC.08 The exit case

A clear strategic acquirer pool, plus a credible path to standalone scale.

Comparable exit: Auth0 was founded in 2013, reached unicorn status by 2019, and was acquired by Okta for $6.5 billion in May 2021 — roughly an eight-year arc. The authorization layer is the next consolidation in the same category, and the buyer pool already exists.

Strategic acquirers who structurally need authorization in their stack and have shown willingness to pay for it:

  • Okta — already paid $6.5B for the authentication half; authorization completes the IAM platform.
  • Microsoft — Entra is positioned for the same role inside the Microsoft cloud; Cisco/Microsoft's joint agent authorization push at RSA 2026 signals appetite.
  • Cloudflare — Zero Trust portfolio is missing the policy layer; Workers and D1 give them the deployment surface.
  • AWS, Google Cloud — every hyperscaler has a half-built authorization story (IAM, Cedar, Zanzibar internals). Buying beats building.
  • HashiCorp (now IBM) — security/policy adjacency to Vault and Boundary.

Standalone path: the Datadog / Snowflake / MongoDB pattern — open-core infrastructure that becomes the default for its category and goes public. The IAM market grows ~$17B between now and 2030; capturing 5% of the authorization half is a $1B+ ARR business.

SEC.09 How we could be wrong

The bear cases — and how we've thought about them.

We've spent more time than we'd like analyzing the cases where the thesis breaks. The honest summary: each one has a real argument, and each one has a real answer.

"A hyperscaler productizes their internal authorization layer."

AWS has Cedar. Google has Zanzibar internals. Microsoft has Entra. Each has had five-plus years to commercialize a fine-grained authorization product, and none has. The reason is structural: their customers want third-party audit credibility that hyperscalers structurally can't provide ("you can't audit your auditor"), and the segment is too narrow to interest their primary GTM. If a hyperscaler reverses course, our cryptographic audit story is the answer to the audit-credibility problem they can't solve.

"Open-source competition compresses margins."

Same dynamic as Datadog vs. Prometheus, MongoDB vs. self-hosted, Confluent vs. Apache Kafka, Snowflake vs. open data formats. In every case, the managed cloud captured the value despite credible OSS alternatives. Our open-source core is a distribution and trust mechanism — not a substitute for the managed offering.

"The compliance tailwind slows or reverses."

EU AI Act enforcement begins 2026 and ramps through 2027; DORA is already in effect; NIS2 transposition deadlines have already passed. The tailwind is regulatory, not market sentiment — meaning it's slow to start and slow to stop. Even under a regulatory rollback scenario, the security posture floor doesn't drop; the compliance ceiling just stops rising.

"The agentic era is hype that fades."

Possible. Even if it does, every regulated enterprise still needs auditable fine-grained access control. The regulated-enterprise wedge stands on its own; the agentic era amplifies it. We're not betting the company on AI adoption rates — we're betting on the architectural correctness that both buyers need.
